# General Curriculum Mapper — Teacher Enrichment Philosophy V1

**Status:** ACTIVE  
**Project:** General Curriculum Mapper  
**Phase:** 4 — Teacher Enrichment  
**Purpose:** Define how teacher-focused enrichment should extend verified curriculum content without replacing, distorting or contaminating the curriculum source.

---

# 1. Purpose

The textbook is the **curriculum anchor, not the limit of the lesson**.

Every lesson must first respect and adequately teach the vocabulary, language structures, skills and activities required by the curriculum source.

Enrichment should then strengthen, extend and recycle that learning without turning the lesson into a different lesson.

The goal is:

> **Curriculum fidelity + deliberate language development + meaningful speaking practice.**

This philosophy applies across textbook series.

It should not depend on whether the curriculum source comes from:

- Beehive;
- Big English;
- Reach Higher;
- or another curriculum added to the General Curriculum Mapper later.

---

# 2. Position Within the General Curriculum Mapper

Teacher Enrichment is **Phase 4** of the General Curriculum Mapper.

The conceptual sequence is:

```text id="te001"
Phase 1 — Curriculum Extraction & Structure
↓
Phase 2 — AI Curriculum & Linguistic Interpretation
↓
Phase 3 — Curriculum Connections & Vertical Mapping
↓
Phase 4 — Teacher Enrichment
```

Phase 4 asks:

> **What useful teaching opportunities can we deliberately add beyond the textbook?**

The distinction between earlier phases and enrichment must remain clear.

Phase 1 tells us what the source contains.

Phase 2 helps us understand the language and learning contained within that evidence.

Phase 3 helps us understand how the learning connects.

Phase 4 deliberately adds teacher-focused intelligence.

---

# 3. The Curriculum Source Comes First

The content extracted and verified from the curriculum source remains the source of truth for what the book requires.

For every lesson, enrichment should first respect:

1. required textbook vocabulary;
2. required sentence structures and language frames;
3. intended grammar or language functions;
4. relevant stories, phonics, reading, listening, speaking, writing or other skills;
5. required curriculum components;
6. and relevant textbook activities.

Enrichment must support the curriculum.

It must not replace it.

The teacher should always be able to distinguish:

> **What comes from the curriculum?**

from:

> **What has been deliberately added as enrichment?**

---

# 4. Curriculum Floor Before Enrichment

The textbook establishes the **curriculum floor**.

Students should first receive sufficient support and practice to access the required curriculum.

Enrichment comes after or around that floor to strengthen learning.

Therefore:

> **FLOOR FIRST. DEPTH SECOND.**

Enrichment should never silently become additional compulsory curriculum.

A teacher should be able to teach the required curriculum without needing to use every enrichment opportunity.

This protects both curriculum fidelity and teacher autonomy.

---

# 5. Extend Naturally, Not Randomly

Additional vocabulary and language should be closely connected to the lesson context.

For example, if a curriculum source teaches classroom objects such as:

```text id="te002"
door
board
cabinet
window
clock
trash can
```

appropriate enrichment might include:

```text id="te003"
desk
chair
curtain
computer
TV
marker
crayon
pencil case
```

The purpose is not to introduce as many new words as possible.

The purpose is to give students a slightly richer vocabulary that allows them to use the target language more naturally.

Extension vocabulary should therefore be:

- age appropriate;
- immediately understandable or easily demonstrated;
- relevant to the lesson context;
- useful for speaking;
- and limited enough that it does not overwhelm the core curriculum.

---

# 6. Recycle Before Adding

Previously learned language is valuable.

When possible, lessons should deliberately reuse vocabulary and sentence structures from:

- earlier pages;
- earlier lessons;
- earlier units;
- earlier books;
- earlier levels;
- and other documented prior learning.

Students should repeatedly encounter familiar language in new combinations.

For example:

```text id="te004"
It's a door.
↓
It's white.
↓
It's a white door.
↓
What color is the door?
↓
The door is white.
```

This creates language development rather than isolated lessons.

The General Curriculum Mapper should eventually use Phase 3 curriculum relationships to make this recycling increasingly deliberate.

---

# 7. Familiar Vocabulary Is a Teaching Resource

Previously taught vocabulary does not automatically need to be retaught as new vocabulary.

Instead, familiar vocabulary can become a useful vehicle for developing stronger English.

For example, students may already know:

```text id="te005"
red
blue
green
yellow
```

Rather than spending substantial lesson time reteaching the words, those words can support more advanced production:

```text id="te006"
It is red.

The pencil is red.

What color is the pencil?

The pencil is red.

Is the pencil red?

Yes, it is.

No, it isn't.
```

The enrichment opportunity is therefore often not:

> **teach more vocabulary**

but:

> **help students do more with vocabulary they already know.**

---

# 8. Vocabulary Knowledge Is Not Productive Language Mastery

A student recognizing a word does not mean the student can use it accurately and independently.

A learner may know:

```text id="te007"
orange
```

but struggle with:

```text id="te008"
It is orange.
```

They may struggle further with:

```text id="te009"
What color is it?

It is orange.
```

or:

```text id="te010"
What color is the bag?

The bag is orange.
```

The General Curriculum Mapper should therefore avoid treating previous vocabulary exposure as evidence of complete language mastery.

Enrichment should help move students from:

```text id="te011"
recognition
↓
recall
↓
sentence production
↓
question and answer
↓
short exchange
↓
independent use
```

---

# 9. Speaking Requires Deliberate Practice

Learners may understand considerably more English than they can produce accurately.

Because exposure to English outside the classroom may be limited, lessons should not assume that understanding automatically becomes accurate speaking.

Students need frequent opportunities to:

- hear;
- repeat;
- retrieve;
- manipulate;
- ask;
- answer;
- and independently produce

correct English structures.

Therefore:

> **Repetition and drilling are legitimate and important parts of the enrichment philosophy.**

Drilling should not simply mean mechanically repeating the same sentence indefinitely.

It should progressively develop control and independence.

---

# 10. Scaffold Productive Language

A useful general progression is:

```text id="te012"
Model
↓
Repeat
↓
Substitute
↓
Question / Answer
↓
Guided Production
↓
Reduced Support
↓
Independent Production
```

For example:

```text id="te013"
Teacher:
What color is the door?

Students:
It's white.
```

Then substitute:

```text id="te014"
board → green
cabinet → brown
clock → black
```

Then gradually remove support until students can produce the language independently.

The exact scaffolding sequence may vary according to:

- age;
- level;
- language complexity;
- class ability;
- curriculum demands;
- and teacher judgement.

The principle matters more than rigid labels.

---

# 11. Build Language Progressively

Where appropriate, enrichment should help students move through increasingly productive language.

A useful general progression is:

```text id="te015"
Word
↓
Phrase
↓
Sentence
↓
Question / Answer
↓
Short Exchange
↓
Independent Use
```

Not every lesson needs to reach every stage.

The appropriate endpoint depends on the curriculum and learner level.

The important principle is that enrichment should help language become increasingly **usable**, not merely recognizable.

---

# 12. Full Answers Matter

Where appropriate for the learner level, students should practise answering questions with useful complete language rather than relying only on isolated words.

For example:

```text id="te016"
Teacher:
What is it?

Student:
Desk.
```

may be useful during initial recognition.

But students should progressively practise:

```text id="te017"
It's a desk.
```

Likewise:

```text id="te018"
Teacher:
What color is the desk?

Student:
Brown.
```

can develop toward:

```text id="te019"
The desk is brown.
```

The goal is not to prohibit natural short answers.

The goal is to ensure students receive enough practice constructing accurate English sentences.

---

# 13. Students Should Learn to Ask Questions

Students should not only practise responding to teacher questions.

Where appropriate, enrichment should progressively develop the ability to form and ask questions.

For example:

```text id="te020"
Teacher asks:
What is it?

Student answers:
It's a desk.
```

can later become:

```text id="te021"
Prompt:
It's a desk.

Teacher:
What was the question?

Student:
What is it?
```

and eventually:

```text id="te022"
Student A:
What is it?

Student B:
It's a desk.
```

This helps students move from passive response toward active communication.

---

# 14. Question Reconstruction

Reverse-engineering questions from answers can be a useful enrichment technique.

For example:

```text id="te023"
Answer:
It is a desk.

Question:
What is it?
```

or:

```text id="te024"
Answer:
The desk is brown.

Question:
What color is the desk?
```

This can strengthen:

- question formation;
- word order;
- auxiliary selection;
- question-word use;
- and understanding of sentence relationships.

It should be used at an appropriate level and with sufficient scaffolding.

---

# 15. Target Predictable Language Errors

Enrichment should deliberately reinforce small but important English structures that learners frequently confuse.

For our current learner context, useful examples may include:

- `is / are`;
- `do / does`;
- `is / are` vs `do / does` in questions;
- `a / an`;
- singular / plural;
- `there is / there are`;
- subject + `be`;
- and correct English word order.

For example, learners may produce:

```text id="te025"
Are you play baseball?
```

The lesson should repeatedly model and practise:

```text id="te026"
Do you play baseball?
```

Instead of:

```text id="te027"
There have a clock in the classroom.
```

practise:

```text id="te028"
There is a clock in the classroom.
```

Instead of:

```text id="te029"
I am boy.
```

practise:

```text id="te030"
I am a boy.
```

These corrections should be embedded naturally into meaningful practice rather than becoming disconnected grammar lectures.

---

# 16. Learner-Specific Enrichment Must Remain Separate From Book Truth

Some enrichment may be appropriate because of the specific learner population rather than because the textbook explicitly requires it.

For example, Korean learners may repeatedly experience difficulty with particular English structures.

This information can legitimately inform enrichment.

However, it must remain identifiable as:

> **LEARNER / TEACHER ENRICHMENT**

rather than:

> **BOOK EVIDENCE**

This distinction allows the General Curriculum Mapper to remain curriculum-source agnostic while still supporting specific teaching contexts.

---

# 17. Keep Grammar Concrete

Grammar reinforcement should usually remain concrete and usable.

Prefer:

- examples;
- contrasts;
- sentence frames;
- substitution;
- pictures;
- retrieval;
- correction;
- and repeated production

over long abstract grammatical explanations.

For example:

```text id="te031"
There is one desk.

There are three desks.
```

may be more useful to younger learners than an extended explanation of subject-verb agreement.

The objective is accurate language use.

---

# 18. Separate New Structures Before Combining Them

When enrichment introduces or reinforces multiple language structures, students should usually receive sufficient controlled practice with each structure before being expected to combine them.

For example:

```text id="te032"
What is it?
It's a desk.
```

can become secure before combining it with:

```text id="te033"
What color is it?
It's brown.
```

and later:

```text id="te034"
Where is the desk?
It's next to the chair.
```

Students can eventually combine known language more flexibly.

The sequence should reduce unnecessary cognitive load.

---

# 19. Scaffold, Then Fade

Support should decrease as students gain control.

A possible progression is:

```text id="te035"
Full Model
↓
Sentence Frame
↓
Partial Frame
↓
Visual Cue
↓
Key Word
↓
Independent Production
```

Students should not remain permanently dependent on full sentence models.

The purpose of scaffolding is ultimately to make the scaffolding unnecessary.

---

# 20. Maximize Student Production

Where appropriate, students should produce more target English than the teacher.

Enrichment should create opportunities for students to:

- repeat;
- retrieve;
- answer;
- ask;
- describe;
- compare;
- identify;
- correct;
- personalize;
- and communicate.

Speaking activities should require meaningful use of the target language rather than only recognition of vocabulary.

---

# 21. Use Personalization

Language becomes more meaningful when students can connect it to their own environment and experience.

For example:

```text id="te036"
What can you see in your classroom?
```

may allow students to use familiar classroom vocabulary in a real context.

Other appropriate personalization may involve:

- belongings;
- family;
- preferences;
- routines;
- school;
- hobbies;
- surroundings;
- or experiences.

Personalization should remain age appropriate and relevant to the curriculum language.

---

# 22. Use the Real Environment Where Appropriate

The classroom and immediate environment can provide useful speaking material.

Students may:

- identify;
- point to;
- find;
- count;
- describe;
- compare;
- ask about;
- and locate

real objects around them.

For example:

```text id="te037"
Can you see a clock?

Yes, I can.

Where is the clock?

It's on the wall.
```

This can make textbook language more concrete without requiring unrelated additional content.

---

# 23. Enrichment Should Increase Depth, Not Just Quantity

A common mistake is to treat enrichment as:

> **more vocabulary**

Good enrichment may instead mean:

- more combinations;
- more retrieval;
- stronger sentence formation;
- more questions;
- more accurate answers;
- greater independence;
- more personalization;
- more deliberate recycling;
- more speaking;
- or more flexible use of known language.

Therefore:

> **Extension means doing more with language, not merely adding more language.**

---

# 24. Enrichment Should Remain Age and Level Appropriate

Enrichment should challenge students without unnecessarily increasing linguistic or conceptual difficulty.

A useful enrichment opportunity should generally be:

- understandable;
- teachable;
- relevant;
- useful;
- appropriately scaffolded;
- and achievable within the learner context.

More difficult does not automatically mean better.

---

# 25. Instructional Vocabulary Is Not Automatically Target Vocabulary

Textbooks sometimes use instructional language that is considerably more difficult than the language students are actually learning.

For example:

```text id="te038"
sequence
```

may appear in an instruction during a lesson whose actual target vocabulary consists of simple concrete nouns.

Such instructional vocabulary may need:

- teacher explanation;
- demonstration;
- simplification;
- gestures;
- examples;
- or temporary scaffolding.

It does not automatically need to become lesson target vocabulary.

The distinction established during Phase 1 should be respected during enrichment.

---

# 26. Games Must Serve Language Production

Games can be useful.

However, a game should not be considered valuable merely because students enjoy it.

Where games are used, they should ideally reinforce:

- retrieval;
- sentence production;
- question formation;
- listening;
- speaking;
- accurate language;
- or meaningful communication.

A vocabulary game that only requires students to recognize a picture may sometimes be appropriate.

But where students already recognize the vocabulary, the same game may be more valuable if it requires them to produce:

```text id="te039"
It's a clock.
```

or:

```text id="te040"
What is it?

It's a clock.
```

The activity should serve the learning goal.

---

# 27. Teacher Autonomy

The General Curriculum Mapper should provide:

- curriculum intelligence;
- enrichment possibilities;
- useful language;
- likely difficulties;
- scaffolding options;
- and teaching opportunities.

It should not dictate one correct teaching method.

Different teachers may appropriately choose to:

- use all enrichment;
- use only selected enrichment;
- replace suggested activities;
- add games;
- increase drilling;
- reduce drilling;
- extend speaking;
- use workbook material;
- add external tools;
- or remain primarily with the curriculum floor.

This is expected.

The teacher remains the final professional decision-maker.

---

# 28. Enrichment Traceability

Every enrichment item should eventually be distinguishable by origin.

Useful categories include:

## Book Content

Explicitly supported by the curriculum source.

## Recycled Language

Language deliberately reused from earlier documented learning.

## Extension Content

Additional vocabulary or language deliberately added to deepen the lesson.

## Error-Prevention / Language Drill

Practice deliberately included to address predictable learner difficulties.

## Learner-Context Enrichment

Teaching intelligence based on observed learner needs or the known learner population.

These categories should not be silently mixed.

---

# 29. Example of Traceable Enrichment

A lesson might contain:

```text id="te041"
BOOK CONTENT

desk
chair

What is it?
It's a desk.
```

Then:

```text id="te042"
RECYCLED LANGUAGE

red
blue
green
```

Then:

```text id="te043"
EXTENSION CONTENT

computer
curtain
marker
```

Then:

```text id="te044"
ERROR-PREVENTION / LANGUAGE DRILL

It is a desk.
They are desks.

There is one desk.
There are three desks.
```

The teacher or downstream lesson-generation system should always be able to identify why each piece of language is present.

---

# 30. Relationship With Curriculum Mapping

Phase 4 becomes more powerful as Phase 3 improves.

Eventually the General Curriculum Mapper should be able to provide enrichment using evidence such as:

```text id="te045"
Previous Learning
↓
Current Learning
↓
Future Learning
```

This allows enrichment to become deliberate rather than random.

For example:

- recycle language students encountered previously;
- strengthen language that remains important now;
- and carefully prepare language students will need later.

However, enrichment should not prematurely teach large amounts of future curriculum simply because the mapper knows what comes next.

Preparation should remain proportionate.

---

# 31. Relationship With Lesson Generation

Teacher Enrichment should describe:

> **what useful additional teaching intelligence exists**

rather than prescribing every detail of:

> **how a generated lesson must present it.**

The downstream lesson packaging and generation phases may decide:

- how much enrichment is relevant;
- how it should be sequenced;
- which activities should be used;
- how many examples are needed;
- and how it should appear in classroom materials.

This separation helps prevent Phase 4 from becoming a lesson-generation prompt.

---

# 32. What Phase 4 Should NOT Become

Teacher Enrichment should not become:

- a replacement curriculum;
- an uncontrolled vocabulary expansion system;
- a complete lesson plan;
- a slide-generation specification;
- a collection of random games;
- a grammar lecture generator;
- or a requirement that every possible enrichment opportunity be taught.

The purpose is to provide **useful teaching intelligence**.

The teacher and later phases decide how much of that intelligence should actually enter a lesson.

---

# 33. Enrichment Quality Check

Before accepting an enrichment suggestion, ask:

1. Does it preserve the required curriculum?
2. Is it clearly distinguishable from Book Evidence?
3. Does it strengthen rather than replace the curriculum?
4. Is it relevant to the current lesson?
5. Does it recycle useful previous learning where appropriate?
6. Does it help students produce language rather than only recognize it?
7. Is the language age and level appropriate?
8. Does it provide sufficient scaffolding?
9. Can support gradually be reduced?
10. Does it address useful learner difficulties where appropriate?
11. Does it create meaningful speaking opportunities?
12. Does it avoid unnecessary additional vocabulary?
13. Is the enrichment optional where it goes beyond the curriculum floor?
14. Can the teacher understand why it has been suggested?

If the answer to several of these questions is no, the enrichment should be reconsidered.

---

# 34. Core Enrichment Principles

The working philosophy can be summarized as:

> **BOOK FIRST.**

Protect the curriculum source.

> **FLOOR FIRST. DEPTH SECOND.**

Secure required learning before adding depth.

> **RECYCLE BEFORE ADDING.**

Use familiar language as material for stronger language production.

> **VOCABULARY KNOWLEDGE IS NOT LANGUAGE MASTERY.**

Move students beyond recognition.

> **BUILD LANGUAGE PROGRESSIVELY.**

Word → Phrase → Sentence → Question/Answer → Exchange → Independent Use.

> **SCAFFOLD, THEN FADE.**

Support students toward independence.

> **MAXIMIZE STUDENT PRODUCTION.**

Students should repeatedly use the target English.

> **TARGET PREDICTABLE ERRORS.**

Build accurate patterns through meaningful repetition.

> **EXTEND NATURALLY.**

Add depth that belongs to the lesson context.

> **PROTECT TEACHER AUTONOMY.**

The system supports professional judgement rather than replacing it.

---

# 35. Final Principle

The purpose of enrichment is not to make lessons bigger.

The purpose is to make learning:

- deeper;
- more connected;
- more accurate;
- more reusable;
- more productive;
- and more speakable.

The General Curriculum Mapper should therefore help teachers move students from:

```text id="te046"
I have seen this language before.
```

toward:

```text id="te047"
I understand it.
↓
I can recall it.
↓
I can use it in a sentence.
↓
I can answer with it.
↓
I can ask with it.
↓
I can combine it with other language.
↓
I can use it independently.
```

That progression is the central purpose of Teacher Enrichment.
